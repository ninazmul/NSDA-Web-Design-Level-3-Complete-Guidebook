Unit 1: Practice Negotiation Skills

Unit Code: GU-09-L2-V1
This unit covers the knowledge, skills, and attitudes required to practice negotiation skills. It specifically includes planning negotiations and participating in negotiations.

📋 Overview

Negotiation is a critical workplace skill, especially in web design where you constantly interact with clients, team members, and stakeholders. This unit teaches you how to plan and execute effective negotiations to achieve mutually beneficial outcomes.

💡 Why Negotiation Matters in Web Design

As a web designer, you'll negotiate project scopes, timelines, pricing, design revisions, feature priorities, and technical compromises. Mastering negotiation helps you deliver better work while maintaining healthy professional relationships.

📝 Element 1: Plan Negotiations

Performance Criteria

PC 1.1

Identify Information for Preparing Negotiation

Before entering any negotiation, you must gather and organize relevant information. This preparation is the foundation of a successful negotiation.

Key preparation steps include:

  • Background research: Learn about the other party — their needs, constraints, preferences, and past behavior in negotiations
  • Topic understanding: Have a thorough understanding of the subject being negotiated (e.g., project scope, pricing, timelines)
  • Desired outcomes: Clearly define what you want to achieve and what you're willing to compromise on
  • BATNA (Best Alternative): Know your best alternative if the negotiation fails
PC 1.2

Create Non-Verbal Environments for Positive Negotiating

The physical and emotional environment greatly impacts negotiation outcomes. Creating a welcoming atmosphere sets a positive tone.

How to create a positive environment:

  • Friendly reception: Greet the other party warmly and professionally
  • Comfortable setting: Ensure the room is warm, well-lit, and comfortable
  • Refreshments: Offering tea, coffee, or water shows hospitality
  • Lead-in conversation: Start with casual conversation before the formal negotiation begins to build rapport
PC 1.3

Identify Active Listening Skills

Active listening is the foundation of effective negotiation. It shows respect and helps you understand the other party's true needs.

Active listening techniques:

  • Be attentive: Give your full attention to the speaker, avoid distractions
  • Don't interrupt: Let the speaker finish their thoughts completely before responding
  • Good posture: Lean slightly forward, maintain an open body position
  • Maintain eye contact: Shows engagement and sincerity
  • Reflective listening: Paraphrase what you heard to confirm understanding — "So what you're saying is..."
PC 1.4

Identify Different Questioning Techniques

Strategic questioning helps you gather information and guide the negotiation toward your desired outcome.

  • Direct questions: Straightforward questions that require specific answers — "What is your budget for this project?"
  • Indirect questions: Subtle questions that gather information without being confrontational — "How have you handled similar projects in the past?"
  • Open-ended questions: Questions that encourage detailed responses — "What are your expectations for the website redesign?"
PC 1.5

Check Information for Accuracy

Always verify that the information you've gathered is correct and up-to-date before entering the negotiation. Cross-reference facts, confirm deadlines, and validate assumptions.

🤝 Element 2: Participate in Negotiations

PC 2.1

Agree on Criteria for Successful Outcome

Before diving into specifics, establish what "success" looks like for all parties involved. This creates a shared framework for evaluation.

Example: "Let's agree that a successful outcome means we finalize the project scope, budget, and timeline by the end of this meeting."

PC 2.2

Consider Desired Outcome of All Parties

Effective negotiation isn't about "winning" — it's about finding solutions that work for everyone. Consider the other party's goals and constraints alongside your own.

PC 2.3

Use Appropriate Language

Professional, respectful, and clear language is essential. Avoid aggressive or dismissive language. Use collaborative phrases like:

  • "I understand your perspective…"
  • "How about we explore this option…"
  • "What if we could find a middle ground…"
  • "I appreciate your flexibility on this…"
PC 2.4 – 2.8

Process, Solutions, Agreement & Follow-up

2.4: Use a variety of questioning techniques (direct, indirect, open-ended) throughout the negotiation.

2.5: Document all issues discussed and processes agreed upon by all parties.

2.6: Discuss possible solutions and assess their viability — consider cost, time, resources, and impact.

2.7: Confirm areas of agreement and record them in writing (minutes, email summary, contract).

2.8: Agree on follow-up actions — who does what, by when, and how progress will be tracked.

❓ Questioning Techniques

📌 Types of Questions

Direct Questions: "What is your maximum budget?" — Used when you need specific information quickly.

Indirect Questions: "How do you typically handle project costs?" — Used to gather information subtly without pressure.

Open-Ended Questions: "Can you tell me more about what you envision for the website?" — Encourages detailed discussion and reveals priorities.

Example Negotiation Scenario

🎬 Scenario: Negotiating a Website Project Scope

Situation: A client wants a 10-page website with e-commerce, blog, and contact forms. Your initial estimate is 120 hours, but the client's budget only covers 80 hours.

Step 1 — Prepare: Research the client's industry, review their current website, understand their goals.

Step 2 — Environment: Meet in a comfortable setting, offer refreshments, start with friendly conversation.

Step 3 — Listen: "Tell me which features are most important for your business goals right now?"

Step 4 — Negotiate: "I understand budget is a constraint. What if we launch with 6 core pages and the contact form first, then add e-commerce and blog in Phase 2?"

Step 5 — Agree: Document the phased approach, timelines, and costs. Both parties sign off.

Step 6 — Follow-up: Send a written summary, schedule Phase 1 kickoff, set a review date for Phase 2.

💼 Practical Examples

Example 1: Negotiating Project Deadline

Client: "We need the website live in 2 weeks."

You (Active Listening): "I hear that timing is critical for you. Can you share what's driving the 2-week deadline?"

Client: "We have a product launch event on the 15th."

You (Open-Ended Question): "What if we launch a landing page for the event by the 15th, and complete the full website by end of month?"

Outcome: Both parties agree on a phased delivery — landing page by event date, full site after.

Example 2: Negotiating Pricing

Client: "Your price is too high. Another agency quoted half of this."

You (Empathy + Assertiveness): "I understand budget is important. Our pricing reflects custom design, responsive development, SEO optimization, and 30 days of post-launch support. The other quote may not include these services. Would you like me to break down exactly what's included?"

Outcome: Client understands the value and agrees, or you negotiate on scope — not quality.

Part 2: Written & Oral Questions with Detailed Answers (12 Items)

Basic
Q1: Define negotiation and outline the four key stages of the negotiation process.

Definition: Negotiation is a structured interactive dialogue between two or more parties aimed at resolving divergent interests, reaching mutual agreements, and aligning expectations.

The 4 Key Stages:

  1. Preparation & Planning: Researching client requirements, analyzing constraints, defining goals, and establishing BATNA.
  2. Opening & Information Exchange: Setting the agenda, stating positions, and building rapport.
  3. Bargaining & Problem-Solving: Making conditional concessions ("If you... then we can...") and exploring creative alternatives.
  4. Closing & Commitment: Formalizing agreements in written minutes of meeting (MoM) and contracts.
Basic
Q2: What are the core components of Active Listening? Why is it crucial in client discovery meetings?

Core Components:

  • Paying Full Attention: Looking at the speaker, avoiding phone distractions, and observing non-verbal signals.
  • Showing That You are Listening: Nodding, using brief acknowledgments ("I see", "Understood").
  • Paraphrasing and Reflecting: Rephrasing the client's message in your own words ("So what you need is an e-commerce checkout that integrates with bKash and Nagad?").
  • Deferring Judgment: Allowing the speaker to finish without interruption.

Crucial Benefit: Prevents costly development rework caused by building features the client never requested.

Basic
Q3: Distinguish between Open-ended and Closed-ended questions with web design examples.

Open-ended Questions: Prompt expansive, descriptive answers. They begin with What, How, Why, Describe. Example: "What challenges are your customers experiencing on your current website?"

Closed-ended Questions: Prompt a single-word or binary (Yes/No) answer. They begin with Do, Is, Are, Will. Example: "Do you already have an active domain name?"

Best Practice: Use open-ended questions during discovery to understand requirements, and closed-ended questions during closing to lock in agreements.

Basic
Q4: What is the purpose of preparing an Agenda prior to a negotiation meeting?

An agenda outlines topics, allocated time blocks, and expected decision outcomes. It benefits negotiations by:

  • Preventing discussions from drifting into tangential debates.
  • Ensuring all critical project aspects (deliverables, timelines, payment terms) are addressed.
  • Allowing all participants to review technical documents and prepare data beforehand.
Intermediate
Q5: Explain the difference between "Positions" and "Interests" in a web design negotiation scenario.

Position: What a party says they want (e.g., "I must have the website completed within 10 days for ৳30,000").

Interest: The underlying motivation, fear, or business need driving that position (e.g., the client needs an online presence before an upcoming trade fair, or has a fixed marketing budget).

Application: Effective negotiators explore underlying interests. For example, instead of rejecting the unrealistic deadline, the developer can offer a launch of a core single-page landing page for the fair, followed by full multi-page development later.

Intermediate
Q6: How do you calculate and establish your BATNA and Reservation Price prior to bidding on a freelance project?
  1. Determine Opportunity Cost: Evaluate existing alternatives (e.g., an ongoing retainer contract paying ৳2,000/hr, or dedicated study time for NSDA Level 4 certification).
  2. Calculate True Cost per Hour: Factor in developer time, software licenses (Figma, IDE), hosting costs, electricity, and taxes.
  3. Define the Reservation Price: Set the absolute minimum monetary compensation below which accepting the contract produces net negative value.
  4. Formulate BATNA: If the client demands a fee below your reservation threshold, your BATNA is to politely decline and allocate those hours to your alternative clients.
Intermediate
Q7: Explain the five Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Modes (Competing, Accommodating, Avoiding, Compromising, Collaborating).
StyleAssertivenessCooperationBest Used When
Collaborating (Win-Win)HighHighBoth parties' concerns are too vital to compromise; designing core system features.
CompromisingMediumMediumReaching an expedient resolution under tight deadlines.
Competing (Win-Lose)HighLowDefending non-negotiables (intellectual property rights, safety protocols).
Accommodating (Lose-Win)LowHighFixing minor cosmetic preferences to preserve client goodwill.

Intermediate
Q8: What non-verbal cues indicate hesitation, discomfort, or hidden objections during pricing presentations?

Key non-verbal indicators to watch for:

  • Facial Micro-expressions: Narrowing eyes, tightened lips, furrowed brows upon hearing a fee.
  • Body Shielding: Suddenly crossing arms, leaning backward, or placing a briefcase/laptop between themselves and you as a physical barrier.
  • Fidgeting & Disengagement: Tapping fingers, sudden loss of eye contact, or shifting gaze toward the door.

Action: Pause immediately, acknowledge the cue gently, and ask an open clarifying question: "I noticed some hesitation when we discussed the maintenance fee—what are your thoughts on that figure?"

Advanced
Q9: How should a web designer document and formalize negotiated agreements to prevent disputes under BNQF/NSDA workplace standards?

To establish accountability and legal clarity:

  • Minutes of Meeting (MoM): Send a structured email within 24 hours summarizing discussed items, agreed decisions, assigned responsibilities, and revised deadlines.
  • Change Order Request (COR): When scope changes occur, formalize a written Change Order specifying deliverables, fee adjustments, and revised delivery milestones signed by both parties.
  • Updated SOW (Statement of Work): Amend the project master agreement with version-controlled documentation.
Advanced
Q10: Describe a step-by-step strategy for handling a client who demands unlimited free revisions post-handover.
  1. Revisit Signed SOW: Gently reference the contractual revision clause agreed upon at project inception (e.g., "Per Section 4.2 of our signed agreement, two rounds of design revisions were included and signed off on October 10").
  2. Separate Bugs from Feature Requests: Acknowledge that bona fide code defects within the 30-day warranty will be patched free of charge, but layout or functional modifications represent new scope.
  3. Offer a Structured Solution: Provide two commercial options:
    • Option A: Hourly billing at your standard professional rate for ad-hoc adjustments.
    • Option B: A monthly maintenance retainer including dedicated support hours.
Advanced
Q11: How do you negotiate Intellectual Property (IP) and source code ownership transfer in web design contracts?

Standard IP Contract Terms:

  • Conditional Transfer: Explicitly state that IP rights and production deployment credentials transfer to the client only after 100% of agreed invoices are cleared in full.
  • Developer Pre-existing Code & Tooling: Retain perpetual developer ownership of reusable libraries, base boilerplate scaffolds, and utility scripts, granting the client a non-exclusive license.
  • Portfolio Exhibition Rights: Reserve the contractual right to display screenshots and project case studies in the agency's portfolio.
Advanced
Q12: You discover midway through development that a third-party API required by the client has tripled its pricing. How do you renegotiate with the client?
  1. Gather Fact-Based Evidence: Document the third-party provider's official pricing announcement, API deprecation notice, or fee schedule.
  2. Prepare Solution Options: Do not approach the client with just a problem. Prepare at least two alternatives (e.g., Solution 1: Absorb the new API tier with adjusted hosting fees; Solution 2: Migrate to an open-source self-hosted alternative with an estimate of development conversion hours).
  3. Conduct an Emergency Alignment Meeting: Walk through the cost-benefit analysis transparently, allowing the client to make the executive business decision.
  4. Document via Amendment: Draft and sign an official contractual addendum.
